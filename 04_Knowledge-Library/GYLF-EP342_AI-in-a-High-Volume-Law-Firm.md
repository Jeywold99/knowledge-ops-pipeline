## Overview

In this episode, host Ken Hardison talks with Tim Hiller, a partner at Hiller Comerford
Injury & Disability — a 240+ employee firm in Buffalo, NY, handling 18,000+ Social
Security disability clients and 2,000+ VA clients, with a growing personal-injury (PI)
practice. Hiller explains how his firm went from AI-curious to AI-dependent in roughly
six months, driven largely by the ability to build custom software by "talking to"
Claude Code without knowing how to code.

The conversation is a practical field guide: what the firm built in-house instead of
buying, how it handles client-data privacy, how it uses AI to tame voluminous case
records, the guardrails it put around AI output, how it communicates AI to a large
staff without triggering fear, and where Hiller thinks legal technology is heading.
The throughline is an **abundance mindset** — AI as a way to do *more* work and compete
with mega-firms, not to cut headcount.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The last six months were an inflection point.** Hiller calls it "the final nail in
  the coffin for anyone who thinks this is just hype."
- **Build, don't always buy.** Using Claude Code, non-coders at the firm built lead-gen
  calculators, custom CRM dashboards, and a case-screening analyzer in hours — work they
  previously outsourced.
- **Keep client data out unless the agreement protects it.** The firm avoids putting
  client data into AI tools, and holds an enterprise agreement for cases where it might.
- **AI shines on voluminous records.** Federal-court Social Security transcripts run
  1,000–12,000 pages; the firm uses **Eve** to draft federal-court-ready statements of
  fact from them.
- **Verify everything.** Firm policy: never submit AI-generated work without independent
  verification — reinforced by the wave of sanctions against lawyers misusing AI.
- **Stay contractually nimble.** Prefer month-to-month deals (or a 90-day bailout) for
  fast-moving AI tools; they got burned by one long-term lock-in early on.
- **Frame AI as abundance, not replacement.** Redeploy staff (e.g., mailroom staff freed
  by document-sorting AI) rather than cutting them; communicate through employee
  "ambassadors," small groups, and one-on-ones.
- **Just start.** Hiller's core advice to owners: personally play with the tools to learn
  their limits before rolling anything out.

## 1. Meet the guest: a high-volume disability firm

Hiller is a partner at Hiller Comerford Injury & Disability in Buffalo, NY — formerly the
Law Office of Kenneth Hiller, founded by his father in 1999. He has practiced disability
law since 2012 ("it's been in my blood"). The firm runs at significant scale: 240+
employees, 18,000+ Social Security clients, 2,000+ VA clients, plus a small but growing
PI practice.

## 2. The turning point: building software by talking to Claude Code

The shift that changed everything was **Claude Code** — the ability for a non-coder to
build "somewhat sophisticated apps and projects just by talking." For Hiller, this
removed the need to hire out for a whole class of work.

## 3. Build in-house instead of buying

Concrete things the firm built or trained itself, rather than paying vendors:

- **Facebook lead-generation calculators.** A VA disability calculator ("find out how
  much money you could be making") that collects contact info at the end — delivering a
  strong cost-per-case, built in-house.
- **Custom CRM dashboards.** Their CRM (**Smart Advocate**) ships PI-oriented reports;
  as a disability firm they now build any dashboard they need in a couple of hours
  instead of paying for custom development.
- **A case-screening analyzer.** A tool to analyze potential medical-malpractice
  intakes *(transcript: "MedMal" — confirm)*, a historically hard-to-systematize,
  attorney-dependent review they expect to improve over time.

## 4. Client-data privacy and enterprise agreements

For everything above, the firm deliberately keeps **client data out** of the tools. It
also holds an **enterprise agreement**, which would allow client data if needed. Hiller
and Hardison note the key distinction lawyers worry about: a vendor *promising* privacy
is not the same as a binding **enterprise agreement** — and that concern is pushing firms
to build tools themselves so they can trust the data handling.

## 5. Considering an in-house software engineer

Because a non-coder can already build useful tools with AI, Hiller is weighing hiring a
**dedicated software engineer** paired with Claude Code — reasoning that a real developer
plus AI could recreate software the firm currently pays for, potentially recouping the
cost in months.

## 6. Taming voluminous records: federal-court statements of fact

A major pain point: when the firm files a Social Security appeal in federal court, the
agency submits a transcript of everything that happened — routinely **1,000–3,000 pages**,
and in one recent case **12,000 pages**. Attorneys must distill that into a statement of
facts for the federal brief.

The firm signed on with **Eve** and is "really happy" with the results — accurate,
15–20-page statements of fact written at a standard suitable for federal court. Hiller
is emphatic that attorneys still review the output before filing.

## 7. The guardrail: never submit unverified AI output

Firm policy, discussed often internally: **nothing AI-generated is filed without
independent verification of its accuracy.** Attorneys need little reminding — they're
already nervous, and the steady stream of news about **sanctioned lawyers** keeps the
risk salient. Hiller notes that many sanctioned attorneys compounded a reckless use of
AI by fighting back instead of owning the mistake.

## 8. The future of case management

Hiller speculates that AI could eventually **displace the rigid CRM interface**: instead
of navigating fixed screens, all case information could live in an AI system you query
via chatbot. Some AI vendors are moving in that direction; CRM companies may not love it.

## 9. Vendor strategy: stay nimble

Given how fast the space moves, the firm avoids long-term technology commitments —
targeting **month-to-month** where possible, or at least a **90-day bailout clause** on
annual deals. This lesson was learned the hard way: an early long-term AI contract they
now regret and no longer use.

## 10. AI and jobs: an abundance mindset

Hiller reframes the jobs question around growth, not cuts. The firm wants to compete with
mega-firms cost-effectively, so AI helps it do **more**, not shed staff. Example:
**Foundation AI** sorts incoming documents and files them in the CRM, sharply lowering
demand on the mailroom — but those staff are **redeployed**, not let go.

His macro view: predictions of mass legal-industry layoffs assume a cap on how much legal
work exists. "We can always do more" — more clients, more cases — so he considers the
doom predictions premature.

## 11. A billing nuance worth noting

In federal court, attorneys are **paid for their time** — so the firm doesn't frame AI as
"spend less time per case." Instead, AI is positioned as eliminating a pain point,
producing top-quality work, and raising win rates — which makes more money for the firm
without undercutting billable value.

## 12. Change management: communicating AI to a large staff

Hiller's approach to rolling AI out across 240+ people:

- **Constant communication** that the tools matter and are **not** here to replace people.
- **Employee ambassadors.** A respected peer often lands the message better than
  leadership. Example: a top trial attorney introduced **Eve** to PI staff and made the
  point that in personal injury, the **personal touch can never be replaced** — visibly
  resonating with the room.
- **Layered rollout.** Large-group launch → smaller follow-up groups → **one-on-ones**
  with anyone resistant.

## 13. Handling resistance

The firm hasn't had to let anyone go over AI adoption. Hiller's preferred stance: if AI
has raised the productivity bar, **everyone is expected to meet that bar** — you don't
have to use AI, but you must hit the level everyone else is performing at.

## 14. Advice to lawyers: just start using it

Hiller's central recommendation: **become a consumer of AI.** Every firm owner should
personally play with Claude Code to understand what these systems can and can't do — you
can't lead adoption you don't understand. Hardison agrees, describing how he started with
low-stakes personal uses (restaurant picks, trip planning) before trusting it for work.

**A prompting technique they both endorse** (credited to the book *AI Leadership*): give
the model full context on your problem, then instruct it to **ask you one question at a
time** until it has enough to propose several options. Hardison recalls asking for five
approaches to a problem — three were strong, and one was an idea he'd never have thought of.

## 15. Advanced use: financial forecasting

The firm uses AI for **financial forecasting** beyond Hiller's own technical ability. By
feeding in KPIs (it even suggested which KPIs to use), it runs large numbers of
simulations to project cash flow — producing worst-case, bottom-quartile, median, and
top-quartile scenarios over coming months. Something he "would never be able to set up"
himself, now built by talking to Claude Code.

## 16. Closing: user-friendly, and a competitive necessity

Hardison — 70 and self-described as "the worst with technology" — says AI has been a big
time-saver and that "if a guy my age can use it, anybody can." Hiller's summary: "If you
know how to ask a question, you know how to use AI" — and lawyers are trained to ask
questions. With private-equity money moving into the disability space, he frames AI
adoption as essential to competing.
